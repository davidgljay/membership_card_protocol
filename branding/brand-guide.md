# Brand & Framing Guide

*Phase 2.2 deliverable, 2026-08-16. Companion to [assurances.md](./assurances.md), [audiences.md](./audiences.md), [reference-sites.md](./reference-sites.md).*

---

## The governing constraint

**Technology is not the protagonist.** The community is. The card is a tool someone hands them.

This is the single rule that overrides every other instruction in this guide. Club builders are rightly wary of technosolutionism — they want paths forward rooted in resourcing and lineage, not another platform promising to fix things. Copy that centers the software, however elegantly, loses this audience.

The precise objection is **technocolonialism**, not technology. Signal is a nonprofit run by movement hackers and is admired. The billionaire-led giants are not. That distinction is the space this brand occupies, and it's expressed structurally — federated, open source, built for living wages rather than another fortune — rather than argued.

**Test for any sentence:** who's the subject? If it's the protocol more often than it's the community, rewrite.

## Name and naming rules

**The protocol is the Membership Card Protocol.** Legacy names — Mark Protocol, Digital Neighborhood Protocol — are superseded and should not appear in new material.

**The object is a card.** Not a "membership card," not a credential, not a badge, not a token.

**To members, it's their community's card:** *your Boots on the Ground card*, *your running club card*. A member being handed one is never told they're receiving a membership card — they're getting their club's card. The protocol stays invisible at the moment of adoption, which is the point.

**A project of Relationality Lab**, displayed above the header. Not a footer credit.

### Component names

| Term | Public-facing? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **card** | Yes — the core noun | |
| **press** | Yes, with explanation | Good metaphor, needs one sentence: the service that posts and registers cards. Consistent with the printing-press lineage of the card idea. |
| **wallet** | Yes | Self-explanatory; borrows correctly from Apple/Google Wallet. |
| **relay** | Rarely | Technical readers only. Belongs in A8 and the developer path, not in organizer-facing copy. |
| **notary** | Not yet | Data donation is out of MVP scope. Introduce only alongside that feature. |
| **verifier** | Developer-facing | To organizers it's just "checking a card." |
| **policy** | Careful | Means something specific here (the rules governing a card's issuance) that collides with everyday usage. Prefer "the rules for who gets a card." |
| **sub-card** | No | Implementation detail. Never surfaces to organizers. |

**Is there a consumer name for the wallet app?** Deferred. Not needed until a frontend exists. Worth noting that Rhizal set a precedent for naming tools separately from the practice.

## Positioning

### One-liner

> **Let anyone in your community**
> **Create places for your community**
> **Anywhere on the internet**

Set on three lines. The repetition of "your community" is deliberate — it makes the line read as a refrain rather than a sentence, and it puts the community in the subject position twice, which is the protagonist test passing visibly.

**"Places" is doing the work.** Concrete-feeling without being specific, and it carries something "resources" doesn't: somewhere you go, somewhere that's yours. It also lands on Relationality Lab's own language — *"what places need to exist for those relationships to grow and thrive?"* — which makes the protocol read as an answer to a question David has been asking publicly for years.

Deliberately vague, and that's the bet: intriguing enough to keep reading, with concrete examples immediately below. Borne out in test conversations so far. **What must follow within one screen:** the examples. A calendar anyone can post to, a members-only game server, a free-stuff list. The intrigue is only an asset if it resolves fast.

Alternatives to test in wireframes:
- *A secure tool for sharing resources and building trust in community.*
- *A card for your community — so the things you make together know who belongs.*
- *Membership that works everywhere, owned by no one.*

### Positioning statement

The Membership Card Protocol helps communities move from a WhatsApp thread, email list, or Discord server to a set of shared places across the internet. Give your members a card, and it becomes easy to create anything from a calendar anyone can post to, to a community-only Minecraft server. With the consent of your community members, you’ll also be able to collect data that demonstrates your community’s impact.

There’s no new community platform to migrate everyone to, and no complex API integrations to manage. Your group stays where it’s most comfortable. And we can’t watch it: the infrastructure that makes cards work is built so that we can’t see who holds your community’s cards or what they do with them. That isn’t a promise we make. It’s a thing the system can’t do.

**On the data sentence.** Data donation is not in MVP scope and the notary service isn't among the five near-term capabilities. It stays in the positioning statement deliberately: **if it resonates, that's a signal to accelerate building it.** The site is a discovery instrument, and this sentence is one of the things it's testing. Two disciplines follow — the claim stays at "demonstrates your community's impact" and never becomes "gets you funded," and reactions to it should be tracked as a finding rather than treated as incidental.

### What this is not

Not a new community platform. Not a VC-backed tech startup. Not a bid to collect data about you and your community.

## Voice

The voice already exists in David's writing. The Substack post and Relationality Lab demonstrate it better than any description; this section names what it's doing so it can be reproduced consistently.

### Principles

**1. Concrete before abstract.** Lead with Desiree, the line-dancing club, the free-stuff channel, the kid who crossed a line. Never lead with "trust infrastructure." A reader should meet a person before they meet a concept.

**2. Name the structural problem, don't moralize about it.** *"Success at a thing that our world desperately needs means failure."* The diagnosis does the persuading. Skip the outrage.

**3. Be specific about limits.** The assurance register's whole method is claims plain enough to be proven wrong. That extends to the voice: say what doesn't work yet, say what the protocol can't do. This is a credibility strategy, not modesty.

**4. Values are structural facts, not ideology.** Signal's *"we can never be acquired by one either"* does more than a manifesto. Say what's true about how the thing is built and let the reader draw the conclusion.

**5. The reader is competent.** Club builders are running organizations under real constraints. Don't explain community to them. Don't sell them on connection — they're already doing the work.

**6. Warm, not folksy.** Plain sentences, real examples, no cutesiness, no exclamation marks, no "hey there."

**7. Humble about the tool, confident about the argument.** *"Technology that is humble and rooted in the communities it serves."* The protocol is modest; the case for resourcing community work is not.

### Register check

| Sounds right | Sounds wrong |
|---|---|
| "Your members can build things for each other." | "Unlock the power of your community." |
| “The protocol is under development, I'm looking for partners to build this with." | "Join the waitlist for early access!" |
| "A card proves one thing and reveals nothing else." | "Military-grade privacy protection." |
| "This won't build your community for you." | "Everything you need to grow your community." |
| "Anyone trusted can run the infrastructure." | "Powered by blockchain technology." |

## Terminology

### Say this, not that

| Say | Not | Why |
|---|---|---|
| card | membership card, credential, badge, token | "Token" carries crypto baggage; the rest are colder |
| your community's card | our platform | The community owns it |
| the people in your community | users | They're not using a product |
| organizer, club builder | admin, host, creator | "Host" is Mighty Networks' word for a monetizer |
| cryptographically verified | military-grade, bank-level, unhackable | The first is true and checkable |
| federated, run by many independent operators | decentralized, trustless, permissionless | Crypto vocabulary; also "trustless" is false here |
| built to be verified, not trusted | secure by design | Specific beats vague |
| shared resources | Web3 primitives, dApps | |
| in development | beta, coming soon, launching | |

### Crypto and blockchain

**Bury the blockchain. Be open about cryptography.**

- **Never in organizer-facing copy:** blockchain, crypto, Web3, Arbitrum, on-chain, wallet address, gas, token, smart contract, NFT.
- **Fine in organizer-facing copy:** encrypted, cryptographically verified, private by default, signed, no one can read it but you.
- **Fine and expected in technical copy:** the actual primitives, named precisely — ML-DSA-44 signs, ML-KEM-768 encrypts, secp256r1 authorizes registry writes. Precision here earns the trust that vagueness elsewhere would lose.
- **If asked directly:** answer straightforwardly. Card registrations are recorded on a public ledger so that revocation can't be suppressed by anyone, including us. Don't be cagey — being caught hiding it is far worse than mentioning it.

**Explain decentralization as a power question, never an architecture question.** The sushi argument is the model: an ecosystem that structurally resists consolidation. Private equity burned billions trying to build the Chipotle of sushi and failed. That's the register — Mastodon does the same thing with *"instant global communication is too important to belong to one company."*

### Talking about the pre-launch state

David's own framing, which should be preserved: *"This is in development, and I'm looking for partner communities to develop it with. They'll get a high-touch relationship that shapes this around their needs."*

Three rules:

1. **Never imply it's usable today.** No "get started," no "try it now."
2. **Be specific about what's real.** The backend exists. A frontend prototype is 1–3 months out. The card you'd hold doesn't exist yet.
3. **Make the offer relational, not transactional.** You're not joining a waitlist; you're being invited into a design conversation. Rhizal's *"we will work with potential partners to ensure that their security needs can be met"* is the model sentence.

## Visual direction

### Palette

Deep teal + warm sand, per [color_palette.md](./color_palette.md). Teal 800 `#085041` for card faces and chrome, Gray 50 `#F1EFE8` as the page background — warm white, not clinical white. Teal 600 `#0F6E56` for primary CTAs.

The palette reads as library card and transit card: institutional trust without corporate coldness. It should not read as a startup or a crypto project.

**Contrast is a real risk.** Teal 100 on Teal 800 and any grey-on-warm-white pairing need checking against WCAG 2.1 AA before shipping — flagged for step 4.6.

### Imagery

**Photography of real gatherings**, following Relationality Lab. People in rooms together, not product screenshots, not abstract network diagrams, not stock photos of diverse hands on laptops. The strongest visual argument for community software is a picture of a community.

**Card mockups as the hero object.** The card is the one thing that should look designed — a rectangular object with rounded corners you'd keep in a wallet, per [card_design_guide.md](./card_design_guide.md). Physical-card lineage: library card, union card, guild card. Not hexagonal badges, not app icons.

**Diagrams only where they earn their place.** Showing the architecture has resonated in conversation, so one clear diagram of issuance-and-verification is worth building. One, not five.

### Structure

Rhizal's layout with this palette. See [reference-sites.md](./reference-sites.md) for the section-by-section pattern.

**Three destinations, one per audience depth:**

| | Destination | Audience | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Landing page** | Club builders | Recognition, then a conversation. Everything in this guide governs it. |
| 2 | **Assurances page** | Curious and skeptical readers of any kind | The register from [assurances.md](./assurances.md), plain and technical renderings side by side. Reached by link, not by scrolling. |
| 3 | **GitHub repository** | Developers, security reviewers | The README carries the architecture breakdown. The site links out; it does not restate. |

This resolves the developer-path question cleanly. There is **no separate developer page** — the repo is the developer page, which is where a developer would go anyway and where the material can stay current without a second thing to maintain.

It also gives the landing page permission to stay short. Anything conceptually heavy has somewhere to live: assurances go to page 2, architecture goes to the repo. The landing page's job is recognition and a conversation, not completeness.

**Linking discipline.** Each of the three has a distinct register — organizer-facing, claim-by-claim, and technical. Copy shouldn't drift between them, and the landing page shouldn't try to pre-explain what page 2 and the repo exist to say. One clear link is better than a summarizing paragraph.

Whether page 1 needs internal sub-pages or disclosure sections beyond this is resolved in wireframes (3.1).

## Call to Action

> **Moved to [messaging.md](./messaging.md) §Get involved**, where it sits inside the reader journey and the form fields are specified. Retained here as the source note.

Let’s assume an embedded survey for the CTA. This will be at the bottom of the page and anchor linked elsewhere. The page should include a “get involved” section, which calls out:

1. Community Organizers
	a.  People building communities where people regularly gather, connect, and support one another.
	b. I’m looking for partners who can test the protocol, give feedback on its implementation, and potentially participate in its governance.
2. Cybersecurity & Trust and Safety Professionals
     a. People who can help to review the protocol and ensure that it lives up to its assurances.
     b. Additionally, people who can help us design the protocol from the beginning to address potential fraud and abuse.
3. People Building Community Applications
    a. Developers, technologists, and people who can put together a working thing with AI coding tools — anyone who wants to build the places communities reach with their cards: calendars, directories, boards, games, whatever a community needs.
    b. Early builders shape what the protocol makes easy, and what a community can be handed on day one.

*Funders are not listed here.* David sends the site to funders directly; they don't need a call to action, and listing them would tilt the page toward an audience it isn't written for.

This should be followed by a short form which asks for key details. This can generate a post request, I’ll set up the endpoint that receives it.

## Applying this

Every claim in site copy traces to a numbered assurance in [assurances.md](./assurances.md). Every piece of copy passes the protagonist test. Where this guide and a good sentence conflict, the sentence probably wins — but say so, and update the guide.
